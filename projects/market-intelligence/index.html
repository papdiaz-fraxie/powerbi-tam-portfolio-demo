Business question

How large is the addressable market, and where is it concentrated?

An Australian telecommunications client needed a quantified view of the companies that matched its targeting requirements. The work converted a broad campaign brief into an addressable-market estimate and a drill-down by state, industry and employee size.

The original client-facing report identified 37,993 potentially addressable Australian companies.

Why the analysis mattered

A TAM number alone is not enough.

A national total tells a commercial team how large the opportunity might be, but not where to begin. The useful analysis was the next layer: where the market was concentrated and how additional targeting filters would change the available pool.

This created a basis for campaign planning rather than treating every geography and segment as equally valuable.

Method

From client brief to usable market intelligence

The analytical process moved from a broad requirement to a measurable market, then progressively narrowed it into segments that could support a real campaign or data-delivery decision.

1. Targeting briefDefine geography, employee-size, industry and other ICP criteria.
2. Data filteringTranslate the brief into repeatable data rules against the available B2B dataset.
3. TAM sizingCalculate the number of companies remaining after the agreed criteria are applied.
4. SegmentationBreak the market down by state, industry and employee size to expose concentration.
5. Commercial actionUse the findings to decide where campaign resources or further data work should be focused.

Finding 1

The market is highly concentrated.

NSW and Victoria contain 24,193 companies, or 63.7% of the total TAM. Adding Queensland increases coverage to 30,723 companies, or 80.9%.

This means a campaign could reach more than four-fifths of the identified national opportunity by concentrating first on three states rather than distributing effort evenly across eight markets.

Finding 2

Geography becomes the first prioritisation layer.

NSW alone represents 34.1% of the addressable market and Victoria another 29.6%. Geography therefore provides an immediate way to sequence activity before industry, employee-size, technology or other commercial filters are layered on top.

Interpretation

What I would recommend from the analysis

The role of analysis is not to stop at the chart. It should tell the commercial team what the numbers mean and what decision should follow.

1. Prioritise coverage

Start with NSW, Victoria and Queensland when the objective is efficient national coverage. Together they account for 80.9% of the source-derived TAM.

2. Then narrow by ICP

Within those states, layer industry and employee-size criteria to identify where the market remains large enough to justify targeted activity.

3. Measure the trade-off

Every extra targeting rule reduces market size. Compare that loss in coverage with whether the tighter segment produces better campaign qualification or commercial outcomes.

Questions I use in this type of work

Market size versus market quality

  • How much of the total market disappears when an additional filter is applied?
  • Does the remaining segment still contain enough accounts to support the campaign?
  • Are the largest geographic segments also the best-performing segments after activation?
  • Which industries or company-size bands produce engagement but fail to qualify?
  • Which signals, including technology or buying-trigger information where available, improve account selection?
  • At what point does tighter targeting become too restrictive?
Decision framework

Size → concentration → activation → outcome

TAM sizing tells us how much market exists.

Segmentation tells us where that market sits.

Campaign activation tells us which segments actually engage.

Qualification analysis tells us whether engagement translates into useful commercial outcomes.

This distinction matters because the segment with the most replies is not automatically the segment producing the most qualified opportunities.

What I did

The work behind the dashboard

This project demonstrates the analytical process behind the visual rather than treating dashboard creation as the end product.

Analysis & data work

  • Translated client ICP and targeting requirements into practical data filters.
  • Worked with large B2B datasets, including around 1M+ records across broader client work.
  • Calculated addressable-market counts and checked that segment totals reconciled to the overall market.
  • Analysed geography, industry and company-size breakdowns.
  • Investigated technographic and buying-trigger information where relevant to account selection.
  • Used post-campaign analysis to compare engagement with qualified outcomes.

Client-facing output

  • Presented the original analysis in Looker Studio as a client-facing market-intelligence report.
  • Provided filters for state, industry and employee size so the market could be explored interactively.
  • Used the analysis to support discussion about where campaign resources should be focused.
  • Rebuilt the analytical concept in Power BI Desktop for this public portfolio.
  • Kept client data private while preserving genuine aggregate findings that demonstrate the analysis.

What I would analyse next

Connect market potential to actual conversion.

The next analytical layer is to join the TAM segmentation to campaign outcomes and compare qualification rates by state, industry, company size and other targeting signals.

That would show whether tighter targeting is genuinely improving commercial quality or simply reducing the available market.

Portfolio transparency

Real aggregates, privacy-safe detail.

The 37,993 total and exact state totals are source-derived from the original client report. Detailed industry, employee-size, revenue, technology-readiness and priority distributions in the public dashboard are synthetic/anonymised.

No client company/contact records, credentials or proprietary raw datasets are published.

What this project demonstrates

TAM analysis is useful when it connects data to a decision. This case study shows the full reasoning chain: define the target market, quantify it, identify where it is concentrated, test how additional criteria reduce coverage, and then use campaign outcomes to judge whether narrower targeting actually produces better commercial results.
